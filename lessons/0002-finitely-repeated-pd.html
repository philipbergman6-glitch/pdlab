The Finitely Repeated Prisoner's Dilemma

Lesson 2 · Repetition should rescue cooperation. With a known last round, it doesn't — and the proof is one induction.

~15 min · Prerequisite: Lesson 1 · Reference: Glossary · Mission: backward-induction proof that defection unravels.

Warm-up from Lesson 1 (answer before opening): which two inequalities alone give strict dominance of D, and which one makes it a dilemma?

Dominance: T > R and P > S. Dilemma: R > P, so (C,C) Pareto-dominates the unique NE (D,D).

1. What changes when the game repeats

Play the stage game N times, observing all past actions. Payoff is the sum (discounting comes in Lesson 3). Three new objects:

History ht: the sequence of action profiles in rounds 1…t−1.
Strategy: a function from every possible history to an action. Not "a plan for the game" but a contingent plan for all 4t−1 histories at each t. "Tit-for-Tat" is a strategy; "cooperate" is not.
Subgame: the game that starts after any history ht. A repeated game has one subgame per history.

Nash equilibrium still applies to full strategies, but it has a loophole: a strategy can prescribe absurd behaviour at histories that are never reached in equilibrium, and NE never checks those. That's what the next concept removes [MIT 14.12 notes, SPNE].

2. Subgame-perfect equilibrium

Definition. A strategy profile is a subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE) if it induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame — including subgames off the equilibrium path [Osborne & Rubinstein, Def. 97.2].

Every SPE is a NE; the converse fails. SPE is the standard solution concept for repeated games because it rules out threats nobody would carry out ("non-credible threats"). In finite games you find SPE by backward induction: solve the last subgames first, replace them by their equilibrium payoffs, step back.

3. Theorem: defection unravels

Theorem. In the N-round repeated PD (any finite N), the unique SPE is: every player defects after every history.

Proof (backward induction on the round). Round N. Whatever hN is, the subgame is the one-shot PD: nothing later can reward or punish. By Lesson 1, D strictly dominates, so (D,D) is the unique NE of every round-N subgame. Round N−1. Both players know round N yields (D,D) regardless of what they do now, so the continuation payoff P is a constant added to every cell. Adding a constant doesn't change best responses: round N−1 is again a one-shot PD, unique NE (D,D). Induction. Suppose all rounds > t are (D,D) in every subgame. Then the continuation from round t is a constant (N−t)P, so round t is a one-shot PD and (D,D) is its unique NE. Hence all-D at every history. ∎

Stronger fact, same idea: even the weaker NE concept gives all-D on the equilibrium path (off-path threats can be anything). The key ingredient is that the stage game has a unique NE. Stage games with multiple NE can support cooperation in finitely repeated play by rewarding/punishing in the last rounds [Fudenberg & Maskin 1986].

4. Feel the unraveling

Restrict to strategies of the form Grim-until-k: cooperate until round k or until the opponent first defects, whichever comes first; then defect forever. Against an opponent using Grim-until-m, your payoff from Grim-until-k is

k < m :  (k−1)R + T + (N−k)P        (you strike first; they retaliate from k+1)
k = m :  (m−1)R + (N−m+1)P
k > m :  (m−1)R + S + (N−m)P        (they strike first)

Check: u(m−1) − u(m) = T − R > 0 and u(m−2) − u(m−1) = P − R < 0. So the best response to m is k = m−1: defect exactly one round before they would. Iterate and it collapses to round 1.

5. But people cooperate…

Experiments with fixed-horizon PDs show substantial cooperation that decays toward the end — end-game effects exist but not full unraveling. The standard theoretical repairs: (a) uncertainty about when the game ends (Lesson 3 — this is what discounting really models), (b) a small chance the opponent is a "committed" reciprocator, which makes mimicking cooperation rational for most of a long game (Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts & Wilson 1982), (c) bounded rationality. For your report, (a) is the one you'll build on; mention (b) with a citation [SEP, "Finite iteration"].
