The One-Shot Prisoner's Dilemma

Lesson 1 · Why two rational players walk into the worst outcome, and the two inequalities that make it a dilemma.

~15 min · Prerequisite: none · Reference: Glossary · Mission: prove (D,D) is the unique NE and explain the dilemma from scratch.

1. The game

Two players each choose, simultaneously and without communication, one of two actions: Cooperate or Defect. Payoffs are written (row, column):

CD
CR, RS, T
DT, SP, P
Definition (Prisoner's Dilemma). The symmetric 2×2 game above is a PD iff
(i) T > R > P > S and (ii) 2R > T + S.

Mnemonic: Temptation, Reward, Punishment, Sucker. Canonical numbers: 5, 3, 1, 0. The "prisoners" story (two suspects, confess or stay silent) is just one instantiation; the structure is what matters, and it recurs in arms races, price wars, climate negotiation, and doping in sport [SEP §1].

2. Dominance: the two-line proof

Definition. Action a strictly dominates b for player i if a yields strictly higher payoff than b against every action of the opponent.

For the row player, compare D against C column-by-column:

opponent plays C:  u(D,C) = T  >  R = u(C,C)     [T > R]
opponent plays D:  u(D,D) = P  >  S = u(C,D)     [P > S]

So D strictly dominates C. By symmetry the same holds for the column player. A player who maximises their own payoff never plays a strictly dominated action — no matter what they believe the other will do. Hence both defect. That's the whole argument; notice it only used T > R and P > S.

3. Nash equilibrium

Definition. A Nash equilibrium is an action profile in which each player's action is a best response to the others': no player can gain by deviating unilaterally [Osborne & Rubinstein, Def. 14.1].

Claim. (D,D) is the unique Nash equilibrium of the PD.

Proof. Existence: at (D,D), row deviating to C gets S < P; same for column. Uniqueness: in any profile where some player plays C, that player can switch to D and strictly gain (D dominates C), so no such profile is an equilibrium. ∎

Dominance-solvable ⇒ unique NE. The converse fails in general: most games have a NE but no dominant strategy. The PD is special precisely because the answer doesn't depend on what you think the other will do.

4. Why it is a dilemma: Pareto

Definition. Profile x Pareto-dominates y if every player is weakly better off in x and at least one strictly. A profile is Pareto efficient if nothing Pareto-dominates it.

Since R > P, (C,C) Pareto-dominates (D,D). Individually rational play lands on the one outcome that is Pareto-dominated by the mutual-cooperation outcome. That gap between individual and collective rationality is the dilemma — and it is what every later lesson (repetition, evolution, spatial structure) tries to close.

5. What is the second inequality for?

Condition (ii), 2R > T + S, plays no role in the one-shot analysis. It says: the total from mutual cooperation exceeds the total from one player exploiting the other. Without it, two players could do better by alternating (C,D), (D,C), (C,D), … than by cooperating every round — so "cooperation" wouldn't be the natural target in repeated play [Axelrod 1980, JCR]. Keep it in your pocket for Lesson 3.

7. Retrieval check

Close the page in your head first. Answer from memory.

Recall prompt: state the uniqueness proof aloud, then open.

Any profile with a C in it lets that player switch to D for a strict gain (T > R or P > S). So only (D,D) survives, and it survives because P > S for both.

Primary source

Read Kuhn, "Prisoner's Dilemma", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, §1–§2 (symmetric 2×2 PD, the two conditions). It's the most careful free treatment and it introduces the notation the rest of the literature uses. Optional: Osborne, An Introduction to Game Theory, ch. 2 for Nash equilibrium formally.
